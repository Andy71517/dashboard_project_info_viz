For Community Bridge, I would prioritize the questions this way:


Q3 — Work and income: highest priority.
This is closest to how we plan employment assistance, emergency aid, and benefits navigation. The chart makes an important practical point: hardship is common even among adults who worked in the past week, especially in lower-income households. We should not use “employed” as a reason to screen someone out of financial-support or benefits-navigation services.
But I need the dashboard to say plainly that this is 2023 household income, not the respondent’s personal earnings, and that “did not work in the last seven days” includes many people who are not unemployed.


Q4 — Loneliness and anxiety by hardship: highest priority.
This is directly useful for referral practices. Adults reporting difficulty with usual expenses also report substantially more frequent loneliness and anxiety symptoms in every cycle shown. That supports pairing financial-assistance outreach with warm referrals to social-support and mental-health resources.
It does not show that financial hardship caused these outcomes, or that any individual client has a diagnosis. The dashboard needs that warning wherever these measures appear.


Q5 — Age differences across states: useful, but secondary.
This can inform outreach materials and referral partnerships: age groups may need different ways to connect with social support. But I would not ask frontline teams to target an individual age group in a state based on a single unstable estimate. The displayed state-by-age intervals are wide in several cases, so the dashboard must suppress or flag unreliable subgroup results and make the national pattern distinct from state results.


Q1 — Change in unemployment since 2019: useful context, not a service-targeting measure.
It helps explain each state’s labor-market backdrop and recovery trajectory. I would use it in a context page for funders and partners, not as the main basis for deciding where emergency assistance is needed. A state can have a comparatively low unemployment rate and still have substantial household expense difficulty.


Q2 — Unemployment and hardship: do not prioritize for decisions in this Round 1 dashboard.
The state-level relationship is weak: the rank correlations range around 0.10–0.21 and the uncertainty bands include zero in every cycle. The scatterplots also show considerable variation in hardship among states with similar unemployment rates. This is a useful caution against equating unemployment with need—not evidence that lower unemployment reliably means less hardship. I would retain one concise “why unemployment is not enough” figure in the methods or interpretation section, but not make it a central interactive view.


What I need next
The main question I would add is:
Where is hardship high, how many people does that represent, and which groups can our programs reach with the services we actually offer?
For that, I need:

A state hardship view with the estimate, 90% interval, valid respondent count/effective sample size, and an explicit reliability flag—not just a rank order.
Population counts or estimated number of adults affected, alongside percentages. A small state with a high percentage and a large state with a moderately high percentage pose different operational demands.
Breakouts of hardship by household income and recent work status within states, only where estimates meet a pre-stated reliability threshold.
A clear missing-data note on every relevant view. Missing income is roughly 14–19% and missing expense responses are 5–11%, with an unexplained change at Cycle 8. We should not present cycle-to-cycle shifts as service-demand changes without flagging that.
Our own operational data: service requests, unmet need, waitlists, benefits-navigation outcomes, referral availability, language access, and local geography. State survey patterns alone cannot tell us where our clients are or whether partner capacity exists.
If possible, county or service-area indicators. State estimates are too broad for deciding where to place outreach events or staff.

I would build the dashboard around household expense hardship first, then use work/income and social-support measures to guide the mix of services—not around the unemployment rate.
