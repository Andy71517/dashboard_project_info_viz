Questions that matter now
Q1 — High priority. This is the clearest workforce-planning question. It identifies where labor-market conditions in 2024 had not returned to their 2019 level and separates unemployment-rate recovery from changes in employment and labor-force counts. I would not call a state “recovered” based on unemployment alone: a lower rate can coexist with a smaller labor force or weaker employment.
Q3 — High priority. The national results show that hardship remains substantial among adults who worked in the last seven days, especially in lower-income households. That is useful for program design because employment assistance alone will not address all financial hardship. But “did not work” is not unemployment, 2023 income is lagged, and income nonresponse is large. This supports coordinated workforce and income-support outreach, not conclusions about the effect of working.
Q4 — High priority. This is directly relevant to coordinated social-service planning. Across all displayed cycles, adults reporting difficulty paying expenses also report substantially higher frequent loneliness and anxiety symptoms. The pattern is stable and the intervals shown are clearly separated at the national level. It justifies screening, referral pathways, and coordination—but does not establish that financial hardship causes loneliness or anxiety.
Questions that matter, but should not drive targeting yet
Q2 — Useful as a diagnostic, not as a state-targeting rule. The dashboard does not show a reliable state-level relationship between lower unemployment and lower expense hardship. The reported state rank correlations are only about 0.10 to 0.21 and every resampling band includes zero. The Cycle 9 scatterplot also shows substantial hardship variation among states with similar unemployment rates.
That tells me not to use a low state unemployment rate as evidence that household need is low, or vice versa. LAUS and HPS describe different state-level phenomena and are only approximately matched by collection period.
Q5 — Secondary priority. The national age pattern is policy-relevant: the displayed results show higher loneliness and lack of support for younger adults, with lower levels for adults age 65 and older. But the evidence shown for individual states has wide intervals and some state-by-age estimates are suppressed. I would use the national pattern to guide broad outreach design, not make state-specific age allocations until the dashboard identifies which state-age estimates meet a stated reliability standard.
What I would dismiss or defer
I would defer claims that Q2 proves labor-market recovery reduced hardship, and I would defer state-specific conclusions from Q5 where intervals are wide or estimates are suppressed.
I also would not treat changes across HPS cycles as a clean trend. The respondent count drops notably in cycles 8 and 9, and missingness changes sharply at cycle 8—particularly for expense difficulty and the social measures. The cause is undocumented. Each cycle should remain separately labeled and interpreted cautiously.
What I need next


For Q1: a sortable state table showing 2019, 2020, and 2024 annual unemployment rates; the rule for “at or below” including ties and rounding; 2020’s monthly peak if that is what “peak” means; and 2019–2024 percent and count changes in employment and labor force. Flag states with lower unemployment but declining labor force or employment.


For Q2: state-by-cycle hardship estimates with 90% intervals, effective sample sizes, and a reliability flag. Show the association as descriptive only, with no ranking of “need” based on unemployment.


For Q3 and Q4: the national estimates are decision-relevant, but I need Virginia-specific results and 90% intervals before using them to shape Virginia outreach or service capacity. For Q3, retain the explicit warning that “worked in last 7 days” is not employment status.


For all state rankings: display uncertainty and a tie rule. The hardship chart makes clear that many state intervals overlap, so a simple ordered list would overstate differences.


My initial planning takeaway is that the dashboard should prioritize Q1, Q3, and Q4, use Q2 as a caution against relying on unemployment alone, and hold Q5 to national or reliably estimated state-level findings.
